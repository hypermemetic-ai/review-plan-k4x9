01 · The experience

One person does one thing, once. Everyone else does nothing.

once
The meeting organizer connects their calendar — one screen, one time. That is how the notetaker knows which meetings to join, and where reviews go.
join
A self-announcing notetaker joins the room with them.
leave
Nothing is asked of anyone.
receive
Within 20 minutes: an email from your domain. The meeting, the date, one button.
read
One click opens their review. Any device, 30 days — then one press resends a fresh link to the same address.

Every page, in the footer: "prepared for <name> — not you? report."

05 · The link

One click on the surface. Every protection beneath it costs the participant nothing.

Unguessablea 256-bit key; wrong guesses see the same "not found" as nonsense links, and mass guessing is throttled and flagged
Nothing to steal from uswe store only one-way fingerprints of links; our logs never contain one
The review travels encryptedbetween our server and the reader's device (HTTPS) — the page and its link are unreadable to the networks in between, hotel wifi and corporate proxies alike
No third parties in the pathclick-tracking off, so the link never crosses another company's servers; no analytics on the page
Preview-proofSlack and Outlook scanners get a neutral "your review is ready" card; a pasted link leaks nothing
Durable on purposelive for 30 days; opening consumes nothing, so scanners can't burn access
"Not you?" kills the linkone press revokes that link, and the event is recorded so it's known

The honest trade — the same one Loom, Fathom, and Stripe make: whoever holds the URL can read that one review. For your clients who want more:

Proposal — decision 6, last slide

The identification toggle. Off: one-click, as above. On — flipped self-serve by any client of yours, for their own meetings — a new device asks once for a six-digit code sent to the participant's own inbox, then remembers it ~30 days. Stronger than a password in practice: it proves the inbox, which also defeats forwarding. The friction lands only on whoever chose it.

06 · Email & infrastructure

Reaching inboxes is a discipline. Staying boring is another.

The email path

  • Postmark carries the mail. A transactional-only sender that refuses bulk marketers; independent tests rank its inbox placement first. Your mail rides that reputation, in its own stream.
  • Your domain, insulated. Sending happens on a subdomain, reviews.your-domain.com — your company email is untouched.
  • Three lines of text, pasted once. Email claiming your name must prove it, or Gmail and Outlook treat it as an imposter. We generate the proof and send it ready to paste; whoever manages your domain adds it — ten minutes, once — and review mail is authorized to carry your name. Prefer no DNS step? Mail can ride our established review domain instead — your brand in the sender name, though the address isn't yours; we recommend the three lines.

The infrastructure

  • One application, one database, one region. A footprint a future contractor reads in an afternoon.
  • Documentation is a deliverable. Architecture notes, a runbook per alert, the test suite.
  • Every account transfers to you whole. Ownership is a decision, not a migration.

07 · Risks, named with owners

Every risk here was found before the contract, not after launch.

The full register runs 27 entries. These four shape the design.

Consent is the product's license to exist shared

Operators of this architecture (Otter, Fireflies) are defending class actions; twelve US states require every participant's consent. Our answer is machinery we control: the bot names itself and announces the review's purpose as it joins — every meeting, logged: what was announced, when, to whom. A host script and invite wording exist as offered material, never a required step for anyone. Objections are honored and logged — eject the bot anytime; an individual opt-out means no review, their words excluded. And by architecture, reviews are claimed, never pushed: the email contains no evaluation; reading one is the participant's own act.

Teams can block outside bots from August 2026 ours to detect

Next month, tenant admins get an off-switch for external notetakers. So admission is monitored per meeting from day one: the rate is in your weekly digest, a blocked counterparty is a same-day report, and Zoom's native feed — host-authorized, no bot in the room — stands as the hedge. We can't join where a tenant says no; we will never be silent about it.

EU rules ban emotion-reading at work ours, by design

Reading emotion from workplace voice is prohibited-practice territory — fines to 7% of turnover. Transcript text is explicitly outside the ban, so the pipeline consumes text only, ever, as a contractual invariant.

What a review says: attribution, quotes, tone yours, informed

Transcripts misattribute and occasionally garble; tone can misfire in a hard meeting. That lives inside the review logic — your side of the line, a trade your design already accepts. Guardrails we recommend and have priced (speaker-count checks, quote verification, a style constitution, a skip-and-flag breaker): separately instructed work, not silently in this contract.

The dependency spine: consent per meeting → tenants admitting the bot → calendar-to-email accuracy → your three DNS records → vendor continuity → transcript quality → model availability (softest — it queues).

08 · Testing

Your name rides every email we send. The testing is sized to that.

Before first real sends

  • Hostile rosters, by the thousand. Duplicate names, diacritics, shared inboxes, no-shows. Everyone ends sent or held-with-reason; on an ambiguous roster, even a correct guess fails the test.
  • Breaking it on purpose. Bot denied, ejected, empty transcript, doubled signals, our server down a day — each lands accounted and visible. Never a wrong send, never silence.
  • A staged live pass on Zoom and Teams. Waiting rooms, the never-admitted bot, two identical names, a mid-meeting rename, a dial-in — recorded audio as the voices, so every hazard repeats exactly.
  • Attacking our own links. Mass guessing, cross-link isolation, cache and referrer leaks, logs, scanner storms, expiry, revocation — exercised for real.
  • Placement proven before real mail. Seed inboxes, yours included; re-checked weekly through the pilot.
  • The top-of-hour spike. Meetings end together at :00 — tested at 3–5× peak, retry storms proven unable to double-send.

In production

  • The ledger detects silence. Any row late to its state raises an alarm; the watcher is itself watched.
  • A daily rehearsal. A synthetic meeting runs the whole chain into controlled inboxes; each must render its own unique marker. Weekly, the cast includes two identical names — the case that regresses quietly.
  • You stay informed without watching. Alerts when something needs attention; a weekly digest of meetings, admission rate, sends, holds with reasons. Clicks counted, not "opens"; aggregates only.

Recommended beyond this contract: the live pass monthly, drills automated, fuzzing to millions of cases.

09 · Build plan

Three stages. 25–30 hours, capped at 30, inside two part-time weeks.

Spine first — every hour buys delivery integrity before polish. Nothing outside these stages blocks real sends.

StageHoursWhat stands at the end
A — the spine~12Bot → transcript → registry → ledger, thin but end to end; core checks; one page behind a hardened link; mail into seed inboxes. Day-one ask: the three DNS lines.
B — the gatehouse~10The full check set; your logic in as a versioned template; the staged live pass, identity hazards first; placement verified; the demo cut.
C — the pilot~8A ramp on your own meetings: shadow → every send approved → sampled → exception-only. Graduation by signed numbers — 200 consecutive clean sends, zero binding errors; any incident steps back one phase. The daily rehearsal and alerting go live.

Our pilot reviewer checks bindings — greeting vs addressee, the right page. Judging coaching stays yours.
